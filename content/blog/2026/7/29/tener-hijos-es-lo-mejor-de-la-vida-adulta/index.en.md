Before even trying to have a child, my wife and I already knew many parents. Now I know that quite a few of them were tremendously happy. Back then it was not very noticeable.

They told us about not sleeping, the logistics, the endless illnesses, how hard it was to travel and how little a Sunday with children looked like a Sunday without them. All of that was true. What they almost never said with the same clarity was something much simpler: having children had made them happy.

Happiness appeared in photos, maybe, but rarely in conversation. It always arrived surrounded by precautions. «It is the best thing that has happened to me, but...». After the *but* came a list of sacrifices long enough to neutralize any chance that it sounded like a recommendation.

TV shows did not help much either. In many of them, children are the force that turns adult life into a succession of sleep, diapers, arguments and lost opportunities. When the story wants to represent freedom, its characters travel, change jobs, fall in love or start over. When it wants to represent that real life has arrived, a baby appears and the music stops.

Now that Esteban is five months old I begin to understand why we parents speak like this.

Telling someone that having a child has made you tremendously happy is delicate. You do not know if they want to have them, if they can, if they are trying without success or if they just decided that this life is not for them. You also do not want to turn a personal experience into a doctrine or look like one of those converts who, as soon as they discover something, need to recruit the rest.

So you learn to start with the difficulties. It is a kind of liability waiver: before saying you are happy, you prove that you are also tired and that you perfectly understand the price you have paid.

Then there is work. In certain professional environments, speaking with too much enthusiasm about fatherhood can sound like a confession of low ambition. It seems more serious to say that your company, your career or the next project occupy the center of your life. Saying that a child now occupies a large part of it can be interpreted as slowing down.

In the case of women, that suspicion has much more real and unfair consequences. We men can be celebrated for being involved fathers; mothers are still asked, although not always out loud, how much professional commitment they might have lost. It is not strange that many people protect their image of ambition by keeping family happiness in a discreet background.

And then there is the *Zeitgeist*. Boasting about a company, a trip, a house or having run a marathon is perfectly acceptable. Saying that having a child is one of the happiest and most meaningful experiences you have lived sounds conventional, unsophisticated or even suspiciously defensive. As if only someone who needs to justify the loss of their old freedom could say it.

Complaining, on the other hand, is allowed. A father can say he has not slept for six months and nobody thinks he is exaggerating. If he says he had never been so happy, it seems he is romanticizing fatherhood.

The result is a half-told truth.

I have only been a father for five months. I am perfectly aware that I am missing almost all the difficult stages and that it would be ridiculous to present myself as an authority. We have already slept little, argued about absurd things and discovered that leaving the house can require logistical planning comparable to moving. I feel no need to hide that part.

But I can also say that my wife and I are tremendously happy. Not happy every minute nor protected from worry, exhaustion or fear. Happy to have had Esteban. Happy in a new way, quite hard to explain from the outside and much easier to recognize once you are inside.

My impression, limited to the people I know, is that something similar happens to the vast majority of parents. They can be exhausted, have relationship problems, feel overwhelmed or miss parts of their previous life. None of that prevents them from being deeply happy that their children exist. Daily difficulty and satisfaction with the decision are not opposite extremes.

I think this remains true even for many divorced parents where childcare had a major weight in their breakup. The lack of sleep, the unequal division of tasks, the disappearance of couple time or differences on how to raise children can wear down a relationship until it breaks. Even so, if they could go back and keep the marriage in exchange for their children not existing, I suspect almost none would accept the deal.

It is an impossible counterfactual and I cannot know what each person would choose. But I think most would be much more miserable without their children, even if being parents contributed to another important part of their life going wrong. An experience can worsen one dimension of life and, at the same time, make the whole life much more worthwhile.

Part of that happiness may come from something quite simple: caring for another person and being, for many years, the most useful person in the world for them offers a very easy compass to follow. A child needs you to feed them, comfort them, protect them and teach them to live. The specific tasks can be exhausting, but you rarely have to ask yourself why they matter.

It is a much clearer way to give meaning to life than pursuing a successful professional career, traveling the world or accumulating any of the experiences we usually present as the great reward of adult freedom. There is nothing wrong with those things. I have dedicated a huge part of my life to my work, I love traveling and I have no intention of giving up either completely. But they are much harder compasses to read: there is always a better job, a bigger company, another country and a new experience you have not lived yet.

Having a child reduces some options, but greatly increases clarity. You no longer have to build from scratch an explanation of what each day is for. There is a specific person whose life is better because you are there. You partially give up some clichés of the interesting life and discover another one that, precisely because it is so old and so common, we had perhaps stopped taking seriously.

I think we parents hide this happiness for good reasons: caution, respect, fear of boasting, awareness that there is no universal experience. But maybe that caution has a cost.

Many thirty-somethings in my circle have had children after thirty-five. It would be frivolous to attribute this mainly to a matter of narrative. Economic uncertainty, job insecurity, housing, the difficulty of balancing work and life, and finding a partner later weigh much more. Nothing is fixed by telling someone they should have had children earlier when they had no stability to care for them.

But I do not think those are the only reasons.

For years we have received a detailed description of everything you lose when having children and a much more timid explanation of what you gain. We have heard about freedom, sleep, money, travel, sex, career and personal time. Much less about the joy, the love, the sense of purpose or the strange expansion of life that can come from caring for someone.

If you only know the costs well, it is logical to postpone the decision until you feel completely ready. The problem is that this moment may never arrive and biology does not adapt very well to our professional calendars. Some decisions allow decades of rehearsal. This one does not always.

I am not saying everyone should have children. There are people who know they do not want them and build magnificent lives. There are others who want them and cannot have them, or for whom the path involves a pain that those of us who have been lucky can barely imagine. Fatherhood does not grant moral superiority, it does not automatically fix a life and it does not guarantee happiness.

I also do not think we should dedicate ourselves to convincing our friends. Selling fatherhood better does not mean recruiting. It means telling the complete experience.

Maybe it is enough to say both truths in the same sentence: it is very difficult and it has made me tremendously happy.

Before having Esteban I would have liked to hear that sentence without it being immediately disabled by ten warnings. I do not know if we would have decided to have him earlier. Important decisions never depend on a single cause and it is very easy to rewrite the past from the calm of the present. But at least we would have had information that now seems essential to me.

I understand my friends were cautious. Maybe they were trying not to pressure us, not to hurt us or not to seem less ambitious. Maybe they thought happiness was visible and did not need to be named.

I think it does need to be named.

Not so that everyone chooses the same life, but so that those who are trying to imagine it do not only receive the inventory of sacrifices. We parents are terrible at selling having children because we explain the price very well and almost whisper what makes us willing to pay it again.
