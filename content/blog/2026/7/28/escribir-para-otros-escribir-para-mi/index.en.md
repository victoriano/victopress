I have been writing on the internet since 2004. I was fourteen years old and I started with a blog about my high school. At that age, that was almost the same as making a blog about the whole world.

Over the years the topics and places changed, but the motivation did not change much. I wrote about photography in Xataka Foto. Then I had a photoblog in El País. All that happened while I was in college. Later I published a few articles on Medium, but for a long time my real blog was Twitter. I split thousands of ideas into smaller and smaller pieces, until I gathered more than 30,000 people on the other side.

Now I see it quite clearly. I always wrote so that someone would do something.

I wanted people to read my work, of course, but not out of a noble calling to contribute to the public conversation. I wrote because I needed to get dates, make money and make friends. I could make those reasons sound noble now, but that is the truth. And it is also true that I achieved all three things.

Writing publicly led me, directly or indirectly, to meet my wife, many of my current best friends and practically every single one of the dozens of people I have hired over these years at Graphext. Seen this way, writing on the internet has not been an accessory of my adult life. It has been one of its invisible infrastructures.

There was also another motivation, something harder to explain. I wrote to force myself to have a more interesting life. If you want to have something to tell, you have to pay more attention, move and go out to find it. A blog was a kind of contract with the version of myself I wanted to be. First you build a character, and then you try to live up to it.

I think it worked. I do not know how many things I did because I really wanted to do them and how many because I sensed that one day I could tell them. The difference probably does not matter much. The story also pushed the life.

I wrote outwards. And that forced me to live outwards.

Now I am thirty-six years old. I am married, Esteban was born five months ago and I am doing very well professionally. It does not mean that I no longer want to be liked, make more money or meet interesting people. It means that that engine no longer pulls me with the same force. For the first time, writing again does not respond to a strategy or an urgent need for something to happen on the other side of the screen.

I want to write so I can read myself when I am seventy years old.

Writing for that future reader also solves a problem that I notice more and more as I get older. Many times I discard an idea before writing it because it does not seem very original to me. Surely someone has already thought of it, explained it better and written a book about it. Then I decide that it is not worth adding another worse version to the internet.

Something similar happens to me with cinema. I have the feeling of having already seen all the narrative tropes of all the movies. A character appears and I think I know what arc they will have. A scene begins and I immediately remember others from which it seems to descend. Accumulating references helps to understand things better, but it can also become a machine to take away their value. Since I recognize the ingredients, I assume that the dish can no longer surprise me.

If I write for myself, originality stops being the price of admission. The seventy-year-old man who reads this will not care if the thirty-six-year-old was the first to think of an idea. He will want to know which ones mattered to him, how he reached them and what he did with them. A thought does not have to be unpublished to deserve to be kept. It is enough that it is truly mine at this moment.

That gives me back a motivation to write that I had been losing. I do not have to provide a great new idea every time I publish. I can try to understand an old idea on my own. In thirty years, the valuable thing will not be having been original, but having left an honest trail.

That does not turn a public blog into a private diary. As I get older I have learned that not everything that happens to me belongs entirely to me. A family argument, a friend's bad moment or a tension with a coworker are part of my life, but also of theirs. They have not chosen to become supporting characters in my story.

That is why I now value self-censorship much more. The word has a bad reputation because it sounds like fear or a lack of sincerity, but sometimes it is a form of care and loyalty. In the heat of a moment, a temporary intimacy can seem essential to explain what one feels. A few months later it may have been resolved, it may have lost all importance or we may discover that we told it in a deeply unfair way. The published text, however, remains.

I think, for example, of the suicide of a friend. I believe he would not have minded if I wrote about it. But the story of his death does not belong only to him and, of course, it does not belong only to me. It is also part of the grief and intimacy of his family and the friends who loved him. Telling it could help me sort out what I felt. Publishing it could force them to encounter a version of something they might prefer to live outside the internet.

There is the difference between writing and publishing. I may need to write a story without having the right to share it.

Being honest about what I think does not give me the right to expose the intimacy of my family, my friends or my coworkers. I want to write with enough depth to preserve my thoughts, but also with enough caution not to fix others in a moment that was only temporary. There are silences that are not cowardice. They are affection.

The texts I wrote twenty years ago move me in a way that the photographs, videos or tweets from back then do not achieve. A photo preserves what was in front of it. A video keeps how we moved and how our voice sounded. A tweet preserves a remark or a reaction. But a long text keeps something different. It keeps the path my head took to get from one idea to another.

Many of those texts seem naive to me. Some are poorly written. In others I discover someone too sure of things that today I am not clear about at all. That is exactly why they matter to me. A version of me who did not yet know what was going to happen next wrote them. They are not reconstructed memories. They are messages sent from a present that no longer exists.

The strange thing is that I have decided to return right now, when writing text has become almost free.

An artificial intelligence can propose a structure, find arguments, offer counterexamples, correct the tone and return in a few seconds an apparently clear version of almost anything. Bouncing ideas with an AI is an extraordinary way to explore an idea. You can throw a still clumsy intuition at it and receive questions, connections and objections that you might have taken days to find.

But that ease also introduces a new confusion. Reading a well-formulated idea and recognizing oneself in it is not the same as having thought it. Fluency produces a false sense of clarity. It is very easy to end a conversation with an AI feeling that one has reached a conclusion when, in reality, one has only attended a convincing conversation.

That is why I believe that in the age of AI it is more important than ever to synthesize one's own thoughts by hand. Bounce ideas first, yes. Open possibilities, discuss, let the machine ask uncomfortable questions. But then you have to close the chat and decide what remains. What statement you would make yours. What nuance you do not want to lose. What contradiction remains unresolved.

AI can expand the space of what is possible. The final compression belongs to me. If I do not do that work, I do not know if I have thought something or if I have simply recognized a good answer as familiar.

These same ideas have bounced with an AI before arriving here. It does not seem like a contradiction to me. It can help me sort, trim or find a more precise sentence. What it cannot decide for me is what deserves to be remembered. I do not worry that a machine helps me write better. I would worry about reaching seventy, reading my texts and not knowing where its words ended and my thoughts really began.

In that sense, the blog becomes important again not despite the fact that there are now too many words, but precisely because there are too many. I need a place where a conversation stops being an infinite draft and becomes a position. A place to say that after all the questions and all the possible answers, this is what I believe today.

Because writing is thinking. Forcing an intuition to take the form of a sentence requires choosing. Each sentence eliminates some ambiguity. Each paragraph establishes that one thing comes after another and that, for some reason, they are related.

And writing also helps to build the internal story with which we give meaning to our life. Lives do not come with a plot. They accumulate jobs, loves, losses, moves, coincidences and decisions that we only try to connect later. By writing we choose some causes, discard others and draw a continuity between people who, although they share a name, sometimes look very little alike.

Perhaps all that meaning is just a *trucco*, to say it like Sorrentino. It is an artifice that we tell ourselves to be able to live inside our own story. But some artifices do not falsify life. They make it readable.

And then there is Esteban.

Now he is five months old and he still cannot read anything. But I would like him to be able to know me one day in a way that I would have liked to know my parents. I would love to read a text written by them when they were thirty-six years old, before knowing how many of the stories that I already know would end. I would like to know what worried them, what they understood then by being successful, what things they thought they had solved and which ones they did not even know were about to start.

Photographs allow me to see them young. A text like that would allow me to hear them think.

I do not want to write to explain to Esteban how he has to live or to leave him a collection of lessons. It is enough for me to offer him raw material. I want him to be able to know his father beyond the role of father, encounter my doubts and see how I tried to make sense of things as they happened. I want him to be able to read the person I was when he still could not remember me.

Maybe he will never read it. That is fine too. The first recipient is still that seventy-year-old man I hope to become.

At fourteen I wrote for the people on the other side of the internet. At thirty-six I write for a seventy-year-old man and for a five-month-old boy. Neither of them can answer me today. Curiously, that makes writing seem much freer to me again.

And, by the way, writing publicly is still dropping a message in a bottle. Someone I already know can pick it up and open a conversation we had not had yet. Maybe a new person will find it, recognize themselves in some idea and we will end up connecting, as has already happened to me so many times.

I no longer need the bottle to return with an answer to justify having thrown it. But I want to keep that possibility open.

For twenty years I wrote so that things would happen. And they happened. I got dates, I made money and I made friends.

Now I write again so that what happens, and what I think while it happens, does not disappear.
